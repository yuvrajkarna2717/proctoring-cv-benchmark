# Known limitations

Updated at the end of each phase. Status: Phase 4 (2026-11-11), before the Phase 3 and Phase 4 real-webcam sessions.

## Scope of Phase 4 results (attention)

- **No real person has been measured.** Head pose and gaze conventions were checked on one still public-domain portrait run through the app; the classification (on screen, away, repeated looks, closed eyes) only with synthetic samples in unit tests. The smoke test's synthetic camera has no face, so it checks that nothing fires without one. Every tolerance, duration, and count is a starting value (the "balanced" preset) until annotated recordings exist (Phases 7–8).
- **Gaze from iris landmarks is coarse.** The iris position against the eye corners gives a few degrees at best. It degrades with glasses (reflections), small faces (eyes under about 12 px wide are left undecided), strong head turns (the far eye is foreshortened and weighted less), and low light. The calibration removes a constant offset per person, not frame-to-frame noise; the margin around the screen absorbs some of it.
- **The eye model is approximate.** Gaze is corrected for the head pose with one eye model, whose corner-depth constant was chosen on that portrait. On it, gaze came out within 7° (yaw) and 10° (pitch) of the camera, against 33–39° for the head alone. Other faces will differ; the calibration measures the screen in the same units, so a consistent error mostly cancels.
- **A learned baseline is weaker than a calibration.** Without a calibration, the median gaze of the first 10 s of detection is taken as the screen centre, with a default screen size (17° left and right, 10° up and down). It is wrong when the person looks away for most of those seconds, and the default size fits a 14–15" laptop at 50–60 cm, not a large or external monitor. Violations measured against it say "uncalibrated".
- **Calibration measures the browser window unless it is full screen.** The overlay requests full screen; if the browser refuses, the result carries a warning. A second monitor is outside the measured area, so looking at it counts as away (which is intended for an exam).
- **Down is ambiguous.** Looking at the keyboard and at notes on the desk look the same to the camera. The extra margin below the screen (5° balanced, 8° lenient, 2° strict) trades misses for fewer false alarms. Typing activity from the exam page could tell them apart; the lab has none.
- **Eye state while looking down is left undecided.** Lids drop when looking down, which looks like closing. With the head lowered more than 20° below its baseline, EYES_CLOSED does not evaluate; GAZE_AWAY covers looking down.
- **Blinks are undersampled.** At 5 FPS most blinks (100–400 ms) fall between frames, so the blink count is a lower bound. Looks away shorter than about 0.4 s (two frame intervals) do not count as glances.
- **Head pose and gaze can both fire for one turn.** A head turned far to the side usually takes the gaze outside the screen too, so HEAD_TURNED and GAZE_AWAY can report the same episode; a reviewer sees two records with the same times.
- **MediaPipe's blendshape names** (`eyeBlinkLeft`, `eyeBlinkRight`) were not checked against which eye they describe. EYES_CLOSED averages both, so it is not affected; leaving one closing eye out of the gaze could pick the wrong eye for a wink.
- **Head pose distance** comes from MediaPipe's metric space with its default camera field of view, so `distanceCm` is approximate (46 cm for the portrait drawn at 95% of a 720p frame).

## Scope of Phase 3 results

- **No face has been verified yet.** The synthetic camera shows no face, so enrollment, verification of a face, FACE_MISMATCH in the browser, and calibration have not run on real faces. In the smoke test the verifier ran YuNet on every frame (no face found) and SFace on a centre crop of the frame (the `embedWhenNoFace` diagnostic). Enrollment, comparison, the mismatch rule, and the calibration math are covered by unit tests with synthetic embeddings and by one controller test through the real pipeline with a fake engine.
- **No FAR or FRR has been measured.** The Phase 3 exit criterion (FAR/FRR on a documented local test set) needs captures of at least two real persons; see the checklist in `docs/TESTING_GUIDE.md`. Until then 0.363 (OpenCV Zoo's default) is an uncalibrated starting value. OpenCV Zoo reports 0.994 accuracy for SFace on its evaluation set; this lab has not checked that number and webcam conditions differ.
- **Latency is for a centre crop, not an aligned face.** The models do the same work either way (fixed 112×112 recognizer input; YuNet's cost depends on the input size, not on faces), but face alignment from real landmarks and frames with several faces are not covered.
- **WebGPU was slower than WASM** on this laptop's Intel UHD Graphics in every run: verifier engine p50 241–304 ms on WebGPU against 61–103 ms on WASM, in both browsers (headless). The first WebGPU recognizer run took 4 s in a standalone experiment; the warm-up absorbs it (1.8 s in the smoke run). Other GPUs may differ; WASM is the default.
- **Memory**: the verifier worker measured 130–135 MB with fp32 SFace and 130.9 MB with int8bq, the default (JavaScript and WASM heap; Chrome, WASM provider). int8bq saves download size, not memory: its int8 weights are folded to float32 when the session is created. int8 SFace needed 51.2 MB. GPU memory is not observable.
- **int8bq accuracy is taken from OpenCV Zoo** (LFW 0.9942 against 0.9940 for fp32) and from agreement with fp32 on two synthetic inputs (cosine 0.9994 and 0.9912). It has not been compared with fp32 on real faces in this lab, and 0.363 was published for fp32.
- **int8bq loads with two engine-side adjustments**: its graph declares 29 inputs that no node reads, which the engine drops after the hash check, and ONNX Runtime's QDQ pass is turned off for it so the weights fold once. Both depend on the pinned file and ONNX Runtime 1.31.1; a new file or runtime version must pass the same checks (the engine test and smoke check M).
- **Run-to-run spread is large**: WASM engine p50 60.5 ms (Chrome, dev server), 80.3 ms (Chrome, build), 102.6 ms (Edge, build), with machine CPU load 32–43% during those windows; the Edge WebGPU window ran at 84% load, and its switch to WebGPU took 20.4 s (14.1 s warm-up) against 3.2–4.0 s in Chrome. Three runs are not enough to compare browsers.
- **SFace provenance** is still undocumented (opencv_zoo#318, no maintainer answer on 2026-11-11). SFace is cleared for POC benchmarking only.

### Face verification and identity behaviour

- Only the largest face is embedded per frame. With two people in view, FACE_MISMATCH compares whoever appears largest; MULTIPLE_FACES (face landmarker) is the rule for a second person.
- The yaw proxy (nose offset along the eye line) is uncalibrated: the value at a given head angle depends on face shape and camera position. Roll is the eye-line angle in the image, so a tilted camera adds to it. Phase 4 measures head pose properly.
- Brightness and sharpness are measured on the aligned 112×112 crop, so they describe the face, not the whole frame; their thresholds suit that crop size only.
- Calibration pairs within one person are correlated (consecutive frames of one capture), so pair counts overstate the data; the composition (persons, captures, conditions) is reported with every rate. With few impostor pairs, FAR targets such as 0.1% cannot be resolved; the panel says so instead of reporting a threshold.
- Embeddings of different SFace variants are never compared with each other, even int8bq and fp32 (cosine 0.9994 on the synthetic input; int8 against fp32 0.95). After a model change the reference must be re-enrolled; calibration samples are kept per model. References enrolled before int8bq became the default show the model mismatch until re-enrolled.
- FACE_MISMATCH is evaluated only on compared frames. A person who keeps the face out of the verification gate (turned, covered, too far) is not reported by FACE_MISMATCH; an active mismatch ends with `data-gap` after 2 s without a comparison. NO_FACE and later head-pose rules cover those cases.
- The reference template is kept in IndexedDB for this origin until deleted. Browsers may evict IndexedDB under storage pressure; the lab does not request persistent storage. Firefox and Safari storage were not tested.
- A face template is derived from a face and is biometric data in many jurisdictions; it is not a picture, but templates can sometimes be partly inverted. Consent text says templates are stored; production use needs legal review.

## Scope of Phase 2 results

- The automated face-engine numbers are for the detector-only path. Chromium's synthetic camera shows no face, so the BlazeFace detector ran on every frame and the Face Mesh V2 landmark model never ran. With a face in view, the landmark model runs for each face, so latency with a face is still unmeasured: the user's real-webcam session exported no reports.
- On that path the two delegates were within run-to-run variation of each other: inference mean 11.4–14.6 ms on GPU and 11.9–14.8 ms on CPU over the five production-build smoke runs (40–41 frames per delegate and run, 1280×720 input, headless Chrome 156 and Edge 157). This does not show which delegate is faster with a face. GPU stays the default until GPU and CPU reports with a face exist (checklist steps 12–13 in `docs/TESTING_GUIDE.md`).
- In the browser, the smoke test verified NO_FACE on the synthetic camera (confirmation after the minimum duration, display, logging, end when detection stops). With a real webcam, the user reported that NO_FACE and MULTIPLE_FACES are accurate; that session produced no numbers. Hysteresis and the cooldown are covered by unit tests.
- No accuracy is measured. There are no annotated recordings yet (Phase 7), so how often NO_FACE or MULTIPLE_FACES fire wrongly or are missed is unknown, and the thresholds are the plan's starting values.
- All measurements come from one laptop (i5-12450H, Intel UHD Graphics; Chrome 156, Edge 157). Headless Chrome used the real GPU (ANGLE, Direct3D 11).

## Scope of Phase 1 results

- No detection model is integrated yet. The diagnostic engine measures the pipeline: capture, transfer, scheduling, worker overhead, and a cheap pixel computation. None of the Phase 1 numbers say anything about model accuracy or model latency.
- Automated measurements come from headless Chrome 156 and Edge 157 with Chromium's synthetic camera (1280×720 at 20 fps) on one laptop (i5-12450H, Intel UHD). Real-webcam data comes from five manual runs on the same laptop with its built-in camera (1280×720 at 30 fps): before the fixes, Chrome 156 on the dev server (157 s) and Firefox 157 on the production build (49 s); after the fixes, Chrome 156 (104 s), Firefox 157 (163 s), and an 82 s Firefox run with two tab switches, all on the production build. Other cameras and hardware will give different numbers.
- Firefox was tested by hand in three runs, the last two on the fixed build. They confirmed the new average FPS, the "not reported" dropped-frame display, page-visibility and focus logging, and the exclusion of hidden-page timer samples. No main-thread stall occurred on a visible page, and the frame-clock fallback ran only while the tab was hidden, so stall logging and the fallback on a visible page are verified only by unit tests and, for stalls, by the Chromium smoke test. Playwright cannot drive stock Firefox. Safari is untested (no macOS device).

## Measurement caveats

- Machine load dominates results. One run on a heavily loaded machine (other processes; 97% CPU load measured right after the run) recorded a 131 ms mean engine time and a 6 fps frame callback rate, against about 8 ms and 20 fps on a quieter machine. Every benchmark must record machine CPU load during the run (the smoke test now does).
- The diagnostic engine spends about 7–8 ms per 720p frame in the headless Chromium runs (4.85 ms in the headed Chrome session), mostly on `drawImage` plus `getImageData` from a GPU-backed `ImageBitmap` (a GPU→CPU readback). Model preprocessing that reads pixels on the CPU will pay a similar cost; Phases 2–3 must measure it.
- Effective FPS (last 5 s) is quantized to one result per window (±0.2 FPS at 5 FPS). Use average FPS over the run for comparisons.
- Average FPS is measured between an engine's first and last result, so a short window rests on few intervals (7 for the 1 FPS engine in an 8 s smoke window). Compare runs of at least a minute.
- The browser that pays the pixel cost differs. Chrome's `ImageBitmap` is GPU-backed: capture took 0.30 ms and the worker's pixel readback dominated engine time (4.85 ms mean). Firefox copies at `createImageBitmap`: capture took 1.62 ms at 1280×720 and 4.51 ms with the resize to 640 px, while engine time was 0.29 ms. Compare capture plus engine time across browsers, not engine time alone.
- rVFC `captureTime` may be taken at different points of each browser's capture pipeline. Frame age and camera-to-result latency are comparable within one browser; differences between browsers (10.14 ms mean in Chrome, 4.28 ms in Firefox) are not necessarily real.
- Firefox-specific: with the page visible every presented frame got a frame callback (4622 of 4622 in 163 s), but while the page is hidden Firefox keeps presenting frames and runs the callback about once per second, so each hidden second adds about 27 missed callbacks (499 for 18.5 s hidden; see `docs/ARCHITECTURE.md`, browser support); `getVideoPlaybackQuality()` reports 0 frames for camera streams, so dropped frames show as "not reported by this browser"; memory and Long Tasks APIs are missing, so memory is "unavailable" and main-thread blocking is visible only through the event-loop probe and the stall log.
- The first Firefox session's single 973 ms event-loop delay and its 50 missed callbacks match a hidden period of about 2 s: Firefox runs background-tab timers about once per second, and 50 frames last about 1.8 s. That build did not log visibility, so this is the likely explanation, not a confirmed one. Visibility, focus, and stalls are now logged with times, and hidden-page samples are excluded; in the tab-switch run 21 samples were excluded and the largest remaining delay was 19 ms.
- Cross-thread timestamps rely on each realm's `performance.timeOrigin`; differences are normally well below 1 ms but not guaranteed.
- Face-engine memory was measured once (headless Chrome, GPU delegate, no face): 15.4 MB for the face worker (JavaScript and WASM heap, including the model) and 23.3 MB in total. GPU memory (textures, shaders) is not included and not observable.
- `measureUserAgentSpecificMemory()` is Chromium-only, needs cross-origin isolation, and took 12–18 s per measurement. Engine instances that share a worker script URL (both diagnostic probes) are reported as one entry. GPU memory is not observable. Dev-server memory is not representative: the Chrome smoke test measured 41.9 MB against the dev server and 7.37 MB against the production build, and the Chrome real-webcam session (dev server) measured 41.8 MB.
- p99 values from fewer than 100 samples are interpolated between the largest observations.
- Face engine start-up is dominated by GPU shader compilation: warm-up took 3.3–3.5 s and worker spawn to ready 3.8–4.1 s in the smoke runs, against about 0.25–0.34 s for a live switch to CPU. The first camera-size frame after the warm-up took 42–90 ms on GPU (later frames about 10–15 ms). Both are included in the first window after a start; compare runs of at least a minute.
- In one headless session, inference times of 412–621 ms appeared about every 9 frames (about 1.8 s) on both delegates, also with the overlay off and the panels hidden. They did not recur in seven later experiment runs (about 600 frames, including a 70 s run and a run recorded with a Chrome trace) or in the six passing smoke runs: no frame took more than 100 ms. The cause was not found; load from other processes during that session is the likely explanation but was not confirmed. Smoke reports count frames over 100 ms (`framesOver100Ms`) so a recurrence shows up.
- In one headless run right after a build, the face engine delivered no results in two 8 s windows. It did not happen again, and no diagnostics were captured. The smoke test fails if an engine delivers no results.
- The dev server is slower: face-engine end-to-end p95 was 58–59 ms on the dev server against 15.6–36.9 ms on the production build (transport p95 up to 49 ms in dev). Use the production build for numbers.

## Behavior caveats

- Priority yielding only avoids starting low-priority work while higher-priority work is running. It cannot preempt work already running in another worker; parallel workers still compete for CPU cores and the GPU.
- When the frame clock falls back to its timer (rVFC stalled), frame timestamps are callback times instead of camera capture times, and new frames are detected through the decoded-frame counter, or through `currentTime` where the browser does not count camera frames (Firefox). In Firefox the `currentTime` path has run only while the tab was hidden (7 stalls, 1 new frame found); an rVFC stall on a visible page has not occurred there. Fallback ticks and stalls are counted so affected windows can be identified.
- Browsers throttle timers and frame callbacks on hidden pages. In Firefox the frame callback kept running about once per second, so detection continued at about 1 FPS (about 15 frames in 18.5 s hidden), and the frame-clock watchdog recorded 7 stalls for two hidden periods. The event-loop statistics exclude hidden periods, but the missed-callback and frame-clock stall counters do not yet; in that run they read 499 and 7, all from hidden time. Average FPS over a run with hidden periods is lower (4.05 and 0.93 in that run), as the "Page hidden" log message warns. Violations that overlap a hidden period are flagged `pageHidden`, but leaving the tab is not itself a violation.
- Stall detection samples a 100 ms timer, so a stall's start time is known to within one probe interval, and blocking shorter than `stallThresholdMs` (250 ms) appears only in the lag statistics. Stall log messages are limited to one per 2 s; the report keeps the latest 50 stalls with timestamps.
- Chromium's synthetic camera sometimes ends its track. In Edge: once at startup during Phase 1, in two of five Edge smoke runs on 2026-11-10 (once mid-run, once at startup followed by "device not found" on the retry), and once more at startup in Phase 2. In Chrome: once, during the memory measurement in window C of a Phase 2 run. In Phase 3 it happened in 4 of 8 runs (Edge twice, 1.4 s and 2.5 s after start; Chrome on the dev server twice). A bare `getUserMedia` page without app code reproduced it in Edge (track ended after 1.5 s, then "Requested device not found"), so the cause is the synthetic device, not the app. The app reports `TRACK_ENDED` instead of running on a dead stream; the smoke test retries camera start once and stops with the camera's error if the camera ends later. Rerun it.
- Consent is recorded per page session only; there is no persisted consent record or audit trail yet.
- The `?automation=1` hook exposes the controller on `window` for testing. Remove or gate it before any deployment.
- The Content Security Policy is applied to production builds only; the dev server runs without it. Deployments must send it as a header, or the workers (where MediaPipe runs) have no CSP. Without the CSP, the workers' egress guard still refuses `fetch`, `XMLHttpRequest`, `WebSocket`, and `EventSource` requests to other origins, but not other channels such as `import()` of a remote module or WebTransport.

### Face engine and violation rules

- `FACE_COUNT` is capped at `numFaces` (3): a fourth face is not counted. MULTIPLE_FACES (two or more) is not affected.
- MediaPipe Face Landmarker reports no per-face score, so face observations carry no confidence; whether a face counts depends only on the detection, presence, and tracking thresholds (0.5 each).
- Strong head turns (beyond about 80°), faces less than half visible, and faces farther than about 2 m are outside the documented model limits; NO_FACE is expected there and must be recorded as a model property.
- Violation times are quantized to the sampling interval (200 ms at 5 FPS). A violation is confirmed on the first frame at or after the minimum duration: 1595–1646 ms after the onset for a 1500 ms minimum in the smoke runs. Brief events shorter than one interval can be missed entirely.
- The overlay shows the latest result, so at 5 FPS boxes trail a moving face by up to one interval plus processing time.
- Starting detection clears the violation history; export the report before restarting a session you want to keep.
- MediaPipe Tasks Vision 1.2.2 tries to send usage statistics to Google (see `docs/MODEL_LICENSES.md`). The lab refuses the request; the log shows one warning per MediaPipe task.
- Not implemented yet: gaze and head pose (Phase 4), identity verification (Phase 3), object detection (Phase 6), and an event for leaving the tab.

## Model and licensing caveats (for later phases)

- SFace training-data provenance is undocumented ([opencv_zoo#318](https://github.com/opencv/opencv_zoo/issues/318)); SFace is cleared for POC benchmarking only.
- MediaPipe model cards describe AR/entertainment as the intended use, exclude human life-critical decisions, and state that surveillance and identity recognition are out of scope (BlazeFace). Proctoring use needs legal and ethics review. This now applies to the integrated Face Landmarker.
- MediaPipe face models are documented as unsuitable for faces turned more than 80° away or less than 50% visible; strong head turns are expected to produce `NO_FACE` observations.
- Whether Apache-2.0 covers every file inside the `.task` bundle is an open question (public forum thread, no maintainer answer).
- COCO `book` has low published AP (7.3 NanoDet-Plus, 15.0 YOLOX-S) and is not a notebook class; notebook detection is expected to be unreliable until measured.
